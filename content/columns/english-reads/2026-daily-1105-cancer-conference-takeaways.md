# Removing ‘invisibility cloaks’ and safely skipping chemo: new weapons in war on cancer shared at US conference

This page is an independently written reading guide to a science report published by The Guardian on 6 June 2026 and bylined to Andrew Gregory. The original article is linked at the foot of the page. What follows is a guide to what the report says and to the way a conference story is built, written for readers who want to take the English slowly; it is not a translation of the piece.

The occasion was the annual meeting of the American Society of Clinical Oncology, known as Asco, in Chicago. The article establishes the scale in its second sentence: 40,000 health professionals, more than 200 sessions, 2,700 poster presentations, and a theme about turning laboratory findings into treatment that reaches patients. Even a reader who has never followed oncology will recognise the shape of the event, because large medical meetings work the same way everywhere: a handful of results are announced to a hall, and the rest of the field spends the following year arguing about them.

The report is organised as five takeaways, and that structure deserves attention before any single number in it. A round-up is an editorial decision. Someone chose these five findings out of the thousands presented, and the order in which they appear is a judgement about importance rather than a measurement of it. The most quotable result of the week is not automatically the one that will still matter in five years.

The first takeaway is about immunotherapy, the family of drugs that recruits a patient's own immune system against a tumour. These drugs transformed cancer medicine over the past decade, but they fail in many patients, and one reason is that cancer cells can conceal themselves from the immune response. The report describes an experimental tablet, GRWD5769, that removes that concealment — the article's own metaphor is an invisibility cloak — so that an existing immunotherapy drug, cemiplimab, can find the tumour and destroy it.

The evidence sits in one dense paragraph, and it is worth slowing down for. In a trial conducted in the UK, France, Spain and Australia, 83 patients took the tablet together with cemiplimab. All of them had already failed earlier treatment, and in most cases immunotherapy had either never worked or had stopped working. Tumours shrank in 26 of the 83 patients, whose cancers were cervical, bladder, liver, bowel, lung or head and neck. Of those 26, 15 shrank by at least 30 per cent. The study was led from the Christie NHS foundation trust in Manchester.

Professor Fiona Thistlethwaite, the trial's principal investigator, called the result impressive for a drug that is simply swallowed, and then added the qualification that nearly every conference report carries: it is early, and further studies are needed. Two other experimental drugs occupy the same section — ivonescimab, which with chemotherapy extended survival in lung cancer by about 15 per cent, and ozekibart, which in bowel cancer shrank some tumours and stopped others from progressing.

The second takeaway produced the only standing ovation the article mentions. Daraxonrasib, a daily pill, doubled survival time in pancreatic cancer, the common cancer with the worst prognosis. In a trial of 500 patients whose disease had already spread, those who took the pill lived for 13.2 months on average, against 6.6 to 6.7 months for those on chemotherapy, and reported fewer side effects. The trial was led by the Dana-Farber Cancer Institute in Boston.

Two sentences follow the numbers, and reading them together is the real lesson of the section. An oncologist who was not involved in the study described the results as landscape-changing and said she wept when she first saw them. A charity welcomed the same results and then objected that access to trials for pancreatic cancer patients remains appallingly low. Both statements are true at once: a drug that doubles survival changes nothing for a patient who is never offered it.

A third drug in that section works differently. Mezigdomide, added to existing treatment for multiple myeloma, more than doubled the time patients lived without their disease getting worse. It attaches itself to a protein in the body and then behaves like a magnet, drawing in the proteins that the cancer cells depend on and marking them for destruction; with those proteins gone, the immune system is also freer to attack what remains.

The third takeaway runs in the opposite direction, and it is easy to miss because it is not about a new weapon. Millions of women with breast cancer may be able to skip chemotherapy. The Optima trial, led by University College London, followed 4,000 patients with newly diagnosed breast cancer in the UK, Norway, Sweden, Australia, New Zealand and Thailand. Women whose tumours scored low on a genomic test could be treated with hormone therapy alone. One participant told the paper that being able to avoid chemotherapy felt like Christmas, and that nine years on she is well.

De-escalation appears twice. In bladder cancer, researchers led by the Institute of Cancer Research in London added the immunotherapy drug durvalumab to chemotherapy and radiotherapy and found that it cut the risk of the disease returning while sparing patients surgery. A treatment that is not given can be as much a result as a treatment that is, though it rarely earns the same headline.

The fourth takeaway is the negative one, and for a reader learning to handle science news it may be the most valuable. Galleri, a blood test designed to detect more than 50 kinds of cancer, was evaluated in 142,000 NHS patients. The question was whether adding it to standard screening would move diagnoses to earlier, more treatable stages. It did not meet that primary endpoint. The article records the verdict of one delegate without softening it: the trial flopped, clear and simple.

The rest of that section is arithmetic on a large scale. Cancer incidence is projected to rise by 21 per cent, from 165 cases per 100,000 people in 2025 to 200 per 100,000 in 2050. Annual diagnoses worldwide, now about 20 million, could reach 35.3 million by mid-century, which works out at 100,000 a day. Against that demand the report sets a projected shortage of 100 million health workers. One of its authors frames the cause without alarm: cancer is fundamentally a disease of ageing, and longer lives are an achievement that health systems have not yet paid for.

The fifth takeaway concerns lifestyle, and it contains the figure most likely to be misread. Cases of early-onset cancer, in people under 50, rose from 1.82 million in 1990 to 3.26 million in 2019, an increase of nearly 80 per cent, while deaths in the same group rose by 27 per cent. Those two percentages measure different things and are easily collapsed into one in casual retelling — the first counts diagnoses, the second counts deaths, and the distance between them is partly a story about better detection.

Within that section sit two studies of sleep. Together they analysed data on 18 million American adults aged 18 to 50 and found that poor sleep patterns were associated with higher rates of early-onset bowel, breast, uterine and ovarian cancer; in some cases, under-50s with insomnia were three times as likely to be diagnosed within five years. The verb matters. The studies report an association, not a mechanism, and the report is careful to say that researchers are still trying to explain the rise.

The section closes on something gentler. Up to 95 per cent of cancer survivors experience disturbed sleep or insomnia at some point during or after treatment, and more than half report mood disturbance, anxiety or fatigue. A trial presented at the same meeting found that regular gentle hatha and restorative yoga eased those symptoms without medication — a modest claim, stated modestly, which is how most good evidence arrives.

Read as a lesson in handling such a story, the piece suggests three habits. Keep relative and absolute figures apart: doubling survival from 6.6 months is not the same as curing anyone, and 80 per cent more cases is not 80 per cent more deaths. Distinguish conference abstracts from published, peer-reviewed trials, because the first is a claim being tested in public. And watch the verbs — associated with, may be fuelling, could reach — since they mark exactly how far the evidence is being stretched.

Here is an editor-written practice sentence, not a quotation from the original article: Had the trial's primary endpoint been met, the argument for adding a blood test to national screening would have rested on earlier diagnoses rather than on the promise of them, and the delegates who left Chicago disappointed would have had a different story to tell their hospitals.

None of this makes the week in Chicago a turning point. It makes it a snapshot of a field moving on several fronts at once — new ways to expose tumours, a new willingness to leave treatments out, and a widening gap between what medicine can offer and how many people will need it. The article's own structure carries that tension: four sections of progress, one of arithmetic, and a closing reminder that sleep, yoga and the timing of a diagnosis belong to the same argument.

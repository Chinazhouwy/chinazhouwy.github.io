# Are schools a great equalizer? They help kids catch up, but wealthier children still pull ahead

This page is an independently written reading guide to an article published by The Conversation on 4 November 2026. The author is David Blazar, a scholar of education policy at the University of Maryland, writing about research he carried out with colleagues. The original article is linked at the foot of the page. What follows is a guide to the argument and to the way the numbers are built, written for readers who want to take the English slowly. It is not a translation of the piece.

The article begins with a quotation and a date. In 1848 the education reformer Horace Mann described public schooling as the great equalizer of the conditions of men. More than 175 years later, the author notes, scholars are still arguing about whether he was right — and the answer, in this piece, is a carefully qualified yes.

Two bodies of evidence pull in opposite directions, and the opening paragraphs set them side by side. One line of research finds that a child's family background shapes academic achievement far more than school does. Another finds that schools narrow gaps that would otherwise be even wider. Both can be true at once, which is the tension the rest of the article works through.

The classic illustration is a study that followed children in Baltimore in the 1980s. Low-income and wealthier children learned at roughly similar rates during the school year; the gap widened over the summer, when some children read with their parents, visited libraries and went to camp while others did not. The author is careful with this evidence, noting that how much summer learning loss really exists is still an open question. The larger point survives the caveat, though: the differences are already large on the first day of kindergarten, and for a given group of students they then stay about the same size through the school years.

What the new research does is change the question. Instead of asking how much students learn at school, the team asked what students receive, in school and out of it: funding, support services, and the time that schools, government programmes and families put into each child.

The method is worth following, because the conclusion depends on it. The researchers combined ten nationally representative government surveys and tracked 77 kinds of investment, from teachers and tutoring to housing, food and childcare, plus factors with no direct price tag such as the time parents spend reading with a child. They then put a dollar value on each item — valuing a parent's time at the average hourly wage, for instance — so that everything could be added up and compared across families.

The total is striking in its ordinariness. Counting government spending, family spending and parental time together, the average child in the United States receives about $502,000 between birth and the age of 18.

The gaps start early. Before the age of five, children in the poorest quarter of families receive roughly $44,000 less in total investment than children in the richest quarter, a difference of 24 per cent, and most of that early shortfall comes from housing and childcare costs.

Then comes the finding that gives the article its shape. As children move into elementary school, the investment gap between the richest and poorest quarters is cut in half, from 24 per cent to 12 per cent. For Black children the effect is larger still: the gap between Black and white children shrinks from 20 per cent in the early years to 7 per cent once kindergarten begins.

That matters because spending is not decorative. Research cited in the piece shows that more school spending improves achievement and long-term outcomes, especially for students from low-income families. Public education, on this account, really does act as an equalizer — for the years in which children are inside it.

So the puzzle sharpens: if investment evens out at school, why do achievement gaps not close? The author's answer is that money buys different things for different children. Once children are in school, those from the lowest-income families receive 72 per cent more in tutoring and 68 per cent more in special education than those from the highest-income families, and Black children receive more than twice as much tutoring as white children. These services are designed to help students catch up. The article's neat formulation is that a dollar spent catching up is not the same as a dollar spent getting ahead.

Tutoring is the clearest case. It has long been one of schools' main catch-up tools, and since the pandemic schools have leaned on it more heavily than ever. The evidence for it is genuinely strong: studies that assign students to tutoring at random find some of the largest test-score gains of any school programme. Federal money made a bigger push possible — Congress sent schools nearly $190 billion, with the largest round distributed on the same formula as the long-standing Title I funding for high-poverty districts — and by 2022-23 more than a third of public schools offered high-dosage tutoring, meaning frequent sessions in small groups.

But for many low-income, Black and Latino students, tutoring was not new. In the study's data, collected between 2014 and 2021, about 17 per cent of school-age children were being tutored, including 25 per cent of Black children and 19 per cent of Latino children, against 14 per cent of white children. Among Black, Latino and low-income children who received tutoring, more than three-quarters got that support from public schools.

The results of all this effort are sobering. Early in the recovery the share of students being tutored did not appear to grow much; a nationally representative survey of parents in autumn 2022 put it at about 15 per cent. Meanwhile the pandemic widened achievement gaps, and students in high-poverty districts still trail those in wealthier districts by more than students in the same grades did before 2020. Federal relief money helped, but it did not restore the earlier position.

Catch-up help is only half the picture, and the second half is where the argument lands. Wealthier children also receive more of the resources that help them pull ahead. Low-income students and students of colour are more likely than richer, white students to have newer, less experienced teachers, a pattern the study documents as well as earlier work. Outside school the differences are starker still: wealthier families invest far more in music lessons, sport and other enrichment, and by 2024 the richest tenth of families spent more than 13 times as much per child as the poorest quarter on childcare, education and extracurricular activities — up from about three times as much in the early 1980s.

The conclusion is therefore narrower than the headline question, and more useful. Public education is the main reason the investment gap between rich and poor children shrinks once children turn five. But equal spending is not equal opportunity: much of the extra school spending on low-income, Black and Latino children goes to making up for gaps that opened before they started school, while wealthier children keep collecting the experienced teachers and enrichment that help them move ahead. If schools are to close gaps rather than merely stop them widening, more catch-up help will not be enough on its own. And because the widest gaps open before kindergarten, in housing and childcare where public support is thinnest, the problem cannot be left to schools alone.

For a reader working on English, the piece is a lesson in how a quantitative argument is assembled. Notice how many of its claims are hedged: by some measures, about, roughly, more likely, still an open question. Each qualifier narrows the sentence it sits in, and each is the part a hurried reader drops. Notice also that the article keeps two different quantities apart — what is invested and what is achieved — and never lets one stand in for the other.

There is useful vocabulary in the way the author names his object. "Investment" and "enrichment" turn activities into countable things; "high-dosage" borrows a phrase from medicine to describe frequent small-group teaching; "catch-up" against "get ahead" sets two directions of spending against each other in three words. Comparison structures carry much of the meaning too, as in "more than 13 times as much per child as the poorest quarter" and "up from about three times as much in the early 1980s", where the second half quietly supplies the history that makes the first half alarming.

Here is an editor-written practice sentence, not a quotation from the original article: Even if every district spent exactly the same amount on each child, the families able to pay for music lessons, tutors and summer camps would still send their children to the classroom door with advantages that no catch-up programme, however well funded, can fully undo.

It is worth being explicit about what the article does not claim. It does not say that schools make no difference, nor that money is wasted on poor students; it says that the same dollar does different work depending on which side of the gap a child starts from. Nor is it a study of achievement itself — it measures investment, and then connects that measure to other research on outcomes. That distinction is the one to keep hold of, both as a reader and as someone likely to meet versions of this argument in other places.

The final paragraph pushes the conclusion past the school gate. If the widest gaps open before children arrive, then the institutions that shape those first years — housing, childcare, the support available to parents — are part of the same argument, and pretending otherwise leaves schools doing repair work with tools designed for something else. That is a policy claim, and a reader is free to disagree with it. But the numbers behind it are laid out clearly enough to argue with, which is more than can be said for most claims about equality.

# AI Can Transform the Classroom Just Like the Calculator

This page is an independently written reading guide to an opinion and analysis article published by Scientific American on 17 April 2024. The authors are Michael M. Crow, Nicole K. Mayberry, Ted Mitchell and Derrick Anderson, all of them working in university leadership and education policy. The magazine labels the piece as argument rather than reporting, and the original is linked at the foot of this page.

The article opens on a dispute that anyone near a campus will recognise. ChatGPT and similar tools spread through classrooms faster than institutions could write rules for them, and the argument that followed was heated. On one side stood professors and teachers worried about the future of postsecondary learning and about traditional disciplines, the humanities above all, with headlines announcing the end of the English major.

The authors do not deny that the worry is real, but they begin with a fact that cuts against panic: roughly a third of teachers from kindergarten to high school already report using AI in the classroom, according to a survey the piece cites. Whatever is decided in committee rooms, the technology is in use. Many colleagues in higher education policy, science policy and university design criticise or dismiss generative AI; these four authors take the opposite view and say so plainly.

Their optimism has a shape worth noticing, because it is not the optimism of enthusiasts. They expect AI to follow the pattern of earlier technologies that widened access to learning and improved success. They also insist that AI is not the hero of the story: students and instructors remain the heroes of human learning, with or without the tool. That sentence is doing quiet work, and it is the concession that keeps the rest of the argument credible.

The historical claim comes next. From the printing press to online mathematics courses, technologies that improve access to good teaching have been dismissed by critics and sceptics, and most reliably by the people who hold the reins in the classroom. The pattern is offered as a general law of institutions: insiders defend the existing arrangement, and the defence is often sincere.

Then comes the case the title promises. In the mid-1970s, Mathematics Teacher magazine surveyed its readers, mainly teachers and mathematicians, and found that 72 per cent of respondents opposed equipping seventh graders with calculators. Science News reported the result in 1975. The timing matters: handheld calculators were only then becoming cheap enough for some schools to consider one device per student.

The objection was not stupid. Educators feared that dependence on a machine would erode students' mathematical skills, and one professor quoted at the time asked what pupils would do when the battery ran out. Read that question in 2024 and it sounds quaint; read it as a statement about fragile competence and it sounds like a question being asked today about writing.

The authors draw the parallel explicitly. They point to a College Board article that looked back on what it called the great calculator panic of the 1980s and 1990s, and they set beside it the current warnings that students who can ask a machine for an essay may never learn to write one, or to answer a prompt on their own. The scenario of a crashed server, in which nobody can compose a paragraph, is the battery question in new clothes.

Having set up the analogy, the authors turn on the terms of the debate. Arguments about essay integrity, they say, are narrow, and so are predictions of falling learning quality, because both measure the new tool against the assignment as it happens to be set today. The wider question is how curriculum, instruction and assessment might change together, which is what happened the last time a calculating device entered the room.

The middle of the piece is a short history lesson. Calculators did not kill mathematics education; the subject's scope widened, and the arrival of the device pushed teachers and academics to rethink what mathematics could be taught at all. Today's high school students use, and understand, graphing calculators and computers better than undergraduate engineers in university laboratories could a generation earlier. The authors describe the present mathematics classroom as more dynamic, inclusive and creative than the one that preceded cheap computing, and they clearly enjoy making the comparison.

The same broadening is then promised for writing and the humanities. Because generative AI can adapt to an individual's pace and preferred way of learning, the authors argue, it can offer customised support and make reading and writing more accessible to students with learning disabilities or with difficulties in traditional writing. The claim is plausible and, in the article, unsupported by evidence; it is offered as a possibility rather than a result.

From there the piece moves to assessment, which is where the argument becomes concrete. If calculators once forced schools to reconsider what a mathematics examination should test, generative AI may force a reconsideration of the college essay as the summit of learning. The authors want assignments designed on the assumption that AI will be present, so that the work values creativity, critical thinking and efficient study rather than the reproduction of finished prose.

One paragraph extends this to admissions. The admissions essay is known to perpetuate bias, and the authors ask what would happen if AI allowed universities to reconceive the tools by which applicants demonstrate aptitude, or to match students more accurately to the institution that would support them. These are questions rather than proposals, and the article is content to leave them open.

The conclusion is a call to embrace rather than resist. History, the authors say, teaches that technologies which broaden and democratise learning are worth taking up, and the real difficulty is not change itself but using these tools to build curricula that serve every learner. Generative AI, on this account, is less a problem to be solved than an ally in making education universal.

It is worth being clear about what the piece does and does not establish. The calculator history is an analogy, not a study of AI and learning, and every prediction about the humanities is stated in the conditional. The one hard number in the article, the 72 per cent of 1975, measures the mood of a profession, not the effect of a technology. The argument is best read as a case for designing assignments deliberately, made by four people who would rather shape the change than police it.

Here is an editor-written practice sentence, not a quotation from the original article: Had the schools of the 1970s treated the calculator as an intruder to be excluded from the examination hall, the mathematics curriculum might have kept its old boundaries while the skills employers actually needed moved on without it.

As you read, watch the machinery of the analogy, since it carries the whole argument: a claim, a historical parallel, a restatement of the present objection in the old words, and then a proposal. Note how often the authors hedge — "could", "may", "promises" — and how rarely they cite findings, because an opinion piece earns its authority from the plausibility of the parallel rather than from data. Ask of each sentence whether it reports something or argues something; the two are mixed here on purpose.

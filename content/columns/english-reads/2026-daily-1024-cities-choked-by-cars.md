# Our cities are choked by cars – here’s how experts would fix them

This page is an independently written reading guide to Ajit Niranjan's article for The Guardian, first published online on 8 May 2026. Follow the original-article link below for the publisher's full text.

The article examines why reducing urban car dependence requires more than replacing petrol cars with electric ones. It presents four broad approaches: improve public transport, make streets safer and more welcoming for walking and cycling, connect suburbs to jobs and services, and understand the different reasons people rely on cars.

A central theme is that transport choices depend on whether alternatives work in daily life. A lower fare alone may not persuade people who need flexible journeys, while better routes, safer streets, reliable late-night service or nearby amenities can address more practical barriers. The article also notes that some people, including rural residents and people with particular disabilities, may have few realistic alternatives to driving.

The discussion connects street design with the allocation of shared urban space. Bike lanes, pedestrian areas and green spaces can make other ways of travelling more attractive, but changing road use can prompt opposition. The article describes temporary trials and public consultation as ways to let residents experience changes and to identify concerns before policies are settled.

Here is an editor-written practice sentence, not a quotation from the original article: Cities are more likely to reduce car dependence when residents can reach everyday destinations safely and reliably without driving.

As you read, track how the article moves from broad goals to practical barriers and then to policy responses. Notice the contrast between measures that improve an existing service and changes that reshape streets, and ask whose needs each proposal is designed to meet.

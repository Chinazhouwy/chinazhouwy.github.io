# People who know more about AI art find it less ethical

This page is an independently written reading guide to an essay by Ionela Bara in Scientific American, first published online on 6 March 2026. Follow the original-article link below for the publisher’s full text.

The essay opens in an auction room. In early 2025 Christie’s in New York sold a collection of works made entirely by artificial intelligence — surreal portraits, photorealistic images, cartoon-style pieces — and the sale brought in more than $700,000, above the estimates set beforehand. The event was a first for a major auction house, and it also drew a protest: more than 6,000 artists objected that the models behind the pictures had been trained on copyrighted work without the consent of its creators. Christie’s framed the sale as a demonstration of human agency in the age of AI; the critics saw an industry commercialising labour it had never paid for.

That argument is only the loudest version of a wider unease. A report published in November 2025 found that more than half of the British novelists it surveyed thought AI could end their careers, and many believed their books had already been used to train large language models. Audiences are less settled than the headlines suggest: surveys cited in the essay indicate that many people accept AI as a tool for working artists while resisting it as a substitute for them.

The author, a researcher in neuroaesthetics, starts from a narrower question. Rather than asking whether AI art is good, she asks what changes when a viewer understands how the picture was produced — the datasets, the training process, the prompts. Her hunch is that unease is not a fixed dislike of machine-made images but something that grows with knowledge, and that this difference helps explain the mixed findings in earlier studies.

Those studies had already shown that people rate AI images lower than human ones on creativity, value and emotional depth. What had not been separated was the role of understanding. If dislike tracks ignorance, the story is about unfamiliarity; if it tracks knowledge, the story is about ethics. Telling those two apart is the point of the experiments that follow.

The first experiment worked with a hundred participants and a deliberately narrow visual world. Twenty landscapes and twenty portraits were generated with DALL-E 3 from prompts modelled on the Impressionist painting of Joaquín Sorolla, with instructions detailed enough to keep the output close to his manner. Half of the participants saw the images cold. The other half read a short explanation of how such a system is trained on a large body of art and text and then produces new pictures from new descriptions.

The explanation changed the moral judgement and nothing else. Participants who understood the mechanism rated the same images as less acceptable to make, to profit from or to present as conventional art, and the effect was strongest where money and artistic reputation were at stake. Their aesthetic ratings, however, barely moved. Knowing how the images were made made people think harder about fairness, not about beauty.

A second experiment tested whether praise could buy back that lost legitimacy. Participants were told that some of the works had been exhibited, sold or well received. For people who had learned how the images were produced, the news did not restore moral acceptability, which runs against the usual finding that expert approval and visible success make a thing seem better. Reputation, in this case, did not work as a substitute for consent.

The third experiment looked for a reflex rather than a reasoned view. Using a speeded categorisation task, participants matched AI-made and human-made Impressionist paintings with words such as good or bad, responding as fast as they could across many trials. This group was given no explanation of how the technology works, standing in for an ordinary viewer. No strong automatic preference for either kind of image appeared, which suggests that the resistance documented earlier is learned rather than instinctive.

The practical conclusion is about disclosure rather than prohibition. If moral discomfort grows with understanding, then explaining the machinery — to audiences, gallerists, collectors and regulators — is likely to shape what the art world accepts. Artists who use these tools can help by saying which models, data and prompts they used and where their own decisions entered the process. Being open may invite criticism, but it also gives viewers something firmer to judge.

Here is an editor-written practice sentence, not a quotation from the original article: A viewer who is told how an image was made does not necessarily like it less, but is far more likely to ask who did the work that the image was built on and whether they agreed to it.

As you read, watch how the essay keeps three kinds of claim apart: what earlier studies had found, what these three experiments actually measured, and what the author thinks the results should mean for museums, artists and policy. Notice the verbs of degree — “less acceptable”, “barely moved”, “no strong automatic preference” — since the argument depends on the size of each effect, and remember that this is a researcher writing about her own work, which is a useful place to practise reading evidence and interpretation as separate things.

# ‘This idea that we can now eat our clothes is pretty revolutionary’: how to give unwanted garments a second life

This page is an independently written reading guide to an article by Petra Stock in The Guardian, first published online on 20 February 2026 in the paper’s Change by degrees series on household sustainability. The original text is available at the link below.

The article begins with a piece of furniture almost every reader owns: a wardrobe that will not close. Australians are buying more new and second-hand clothing than ever, and the surplus has to go somewhere. The question the piece sets out to answer is practical rather than philosophical — what should a household actually do with the garments nobody wears any more?

The numbers come first. A report by Seamless, the country’s clothing stewardship scheme, found that about 1.5 billion new garments were bought nationally in a single year, the equivalent of fifty-five items for every person. Of the clothes that are discarded, roughly half go straight to landfill, a little under four in ten are reused locally or exported, and under one in ten are recycled. The landfill share works out at some 229,000 tonnes a year.

Ainsley Simpson, Seamless’s chief executive, supplies the frame for those figures. She describes the overconsumption of clothing as having become normalised, and argues that this has already locked in a climate and water footprint out of step with any circular future: around 14.5 million tonnes of carbon emissions and the equivalent of 1.8 billion tonnes of water. Notice the move being made here. What looks like a private matter of crowded cupboards is being restated as an industrial balance sheet.

The middle of the article turns to a single New Zealand label, Kowtow, which has spent years stripping plastic and petrochemicals out of its production. The point is that this goes well beyond the fabric. Zips, buttons and even the polycotton thread were either designed out or replaced with recyclable or natural alternatives, so that a garment can be described as genuinely plastic-free.

That thoroughness only makes sense once you see what the company wants to do at the end of a garment’s life. Kowtow has begun turning its textile waste into biochar — a charcoal made from organic material that can be used to enrich soils. The scheme has so far converted a tonne of what would otherwise have been waste, and the founder, Gosia Piatek, describes mixing it with soil to grow tomatoes in the label’s own workroom. Hence the strange phrase in the headline about eating one’s clothes.

The loop closes neatly. Returned items have their trims removed and fed back into the repair programme, so repair and recycling are treated as one system rather than two competing services. Tessa Bradley, the label’s head of sustainability, sums up the intention: by unmaking what has been made, the company gives something back, and the end of a garment becomes the beginning of something else.

Set against that, the article offers a picture of ordinary confusion. Seamless estimates that 5.27 billion items are stockpiled in Australian wardrobes, which is 193 pieces for every person in the country. A survey led by RMIT found that most people owned clothes they had not worn for a year, and that a third had not touched half of what they owned.

Simpson’s advice is a ladder rather than a single step: re-wear, repair and share where possible, and donate good-quality items to established charity and resale networks, which extends their life and reduces demand for new clothing. The charity sector adds a test of its own. Matt McMahon of Salvos Stores suggests that if an item is good enough to give to a friend, it is probably good enough to donate — a reminder that donation is not a disposal route for rubbish.

The recycling end of the ladder is the weakest. Textiles have the lowest recycling rate of any waste material, at about five per cent, and most of that is carpet rather than clothing; federal data put textile waste at roughly 33 kilograms per person, with garments the largest component. Tailored schemes remain niche, which is why the article spends time on the exceptions.

In South Australia, state and local governments worked with charity partners on statewide collection drives that accepted clothing, footwear and linens in any condition; a single event recovered almost 20 tonnes of unwanted textiles for resale and recycling. Seamless is also funding pilots, including postal return satchels run with Australia Post and RM Williams, workplace collections, charitable collaborations and local recycling hubs.

The final section widens the lens again. Julie Boulton, a sustainable fashion consultant, argues that the idea of clothes as disposable items has to go, and that the fix is system-wide: producers should be responsible for what they put on the market, and damaging materials should not be used in the first place. She also points at volume — much of what is made is heavily discounted or left unsold, with the true cost of production written out of the price. Her advice to shoppers is deliberately modest: buy what you need rather than what you want, and do not buy on impulse.

It is worth noticing what the article does not claim. A single tonne of biochar is a demonstration, not an industry, and the label behind it is one company among thousands. Recycling, on the evidence given, cannot absorb the volume now being produced. The piece therefore holds two arguments at once — that households should handle their own clothes better, and that better handling will not be enough on its own — without pretending to rank them.

Here is an editor-written practice sentence, not a quotation from the original article: Even if every household sorted, repaired and donated its unwanted clothing perfectly, the volumes described in this report suggest that collection schemes alone could not keep pace with the rate at which new garments arrive in wardrobes.

As you read, watch how the argument is assembled: national statistics, then one company’s experiment, then expert quotation, then policy. Keep the middle terms of that sequence separate — reused, donated and recycled are not interchangeable, and the article’s numbers depend on the distinction. Note also the small verbs of obligation and possibility (“should”, “could”, “suggest”), which mark where the writer is reporting a case and where she is arguing one.

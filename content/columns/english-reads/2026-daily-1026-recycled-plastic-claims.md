# Europe’s supermarket shelves packed with ‘misleading’ claims about recycled plastic packaging

This page is an independently written reading guide to a Guardian investigation by Stefano Valentino, first published online on 27 January 2026. Follow the original-article link below for the publisher’s full text.

The investigation examines plastic packaging marketed as sustainable in Europe. It reports that some packaging described as recycled or climate-friendly may contain only a small share of material recovered from waste, while most of its feedstock remains fossil-based. The article asks whether the labels consumers see reflect the physical contents of the products.

It explains how chemical recycling by pyrolysis turns plastic waste into an oil that can be used as feedstock. According to the article, this oil is mixed with virgin petroleum-derived material before it enters plastic production. The guide’s central distinction is between a process that receives recycled input and a finished package that physically contains recycled material.

The article also describes “mass-balance” accounting, in which recycled input can be allocated on paper to selected output batches. It questions whether this accounting allows a product to be marketed as fully recycled even when its physical contents do not match that claim. A separate “avoided emissions” calculation is discussed as another way reported climate benefits can depend on accounting choices.

The investigation reports criticism from environmental experts and raises concerns about the independence of some reviews. It also notes that companies named in the report did not provide full life-cycle assessments or respond to questions. These points are presented as the article’s reporting and attributed claims, not as an independent verification by this guide.

Here is an editor-written practice sentence, not a quotation from the original article: A recycling label can sound convincing even when the accounting method says more about allocated credits than about the material in the package.

As you read, distinguish physical composition from accounting attribution. Track how the article moves from the recycling process to certification and emissions calculations, and notice which claims are attributed to companies, researchers, or campaigners.

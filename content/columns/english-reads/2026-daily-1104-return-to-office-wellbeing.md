# The Hidden Cost of Calling Workers Back to the Office

This page is an independently written reading guide to a business news report published by The Guardian on 15 July 2025. The reporter is Joanna Partridge, and the paper ran the story as an exclusive. The original article is linked at the foot of this page; what follows is a guide to its numbers and its framing, written for readers who want to follow the English closely.

The news itself is a survey. A recruitment company, Hays, questioned 3,600 employers and employees across Britain between late April and early May 2025, and the headline finding is a figure about mood rather than money: 38 per cent of the workers polled said that recent stories about companies tightening their attendance rules had damaged their wellbeing.

That number is interesting precisely because nothing had yet happened to most of the people reporting it. They were not describing an order they had received; they were describing the effect of expecting one. The report uses the word "fear" in its headline, and the body of the story justifies it, because what such a survey captures is anticipation — the particular strain of waiting to learn whether the shape of your week is about to be rearranged.

To see why the anticipation is live, the article sets out what firms have been doing. In May, HSBC told staff in its UK high street branches that bonuses might be reduced for those who spent less than 60 per cent of their time at a desk. Barclays and Santander have also been tightening their rules. At the start of June, Man Group, the listed hedge fund, asked its London analysts to come in five days a week for a period while it tried to recover from a run of poor results.

Against those moves sits the arrangement most British office workers now have. According to the Office for National Statistics, 28 per cent of working adults in Great Britain were hybrid in the first three months of 2025, splitting the week between home and workplace. The poll puts a satisfaction figure on that group: 84 per cent of hybrid workers said the pattern had a positive effect on their wellbeing, understood to cover mental, physical, social and financial health.

The gender split inside that figure is modest but consistent. 87 per cent of the women who work hybrid said it had improved their wellbeing, against 80 per cent of the men. When the question turned to the news about mandates, the gap widened: 42 per cent of women said those stories had harmed their wellbeing, against 32 per cent of men. Age divided the sample as well, with workers in their twenties more concerned than colleagues over fifty.

Money runs underneath nearly all of it. Asked what would make them reluctant to spend more days in the office, 59 per cent pointed to their finances, and the obvious reason is the commute: fares, coffee, lunch, the small daily expenditures that a week at home quietly removes. A wellbeing survey that ignored cost would be measuring the wrong thing, and this one does not ignore it.

The article gives the closing word on that point to Hannah Pearsall, Hays's head of wellbeing. Her warning, paraphrased rather than quoted here, is that employers underestimate what a return-to-office push does to staff, financial wellbeing included, and that the consequences could be serious for the business itself. The word she reaches for is "catastrophic", and it is worth noticing that the word is hers, not the reporter's.

Read as reporting, the story is careful about where each fact comes from: the percentages belong to Hays, the hybrid share belongs to the ONS, and the corporate examples belong to earlier coverage. What the article does not do is test any of it. No company is shown losing staff, no productivity figure moves in either direction, and no researcher appears to dispute the poll. A reader who wants the other side of the argument has to supply it.

That missing side is not hard to reconstruct. Employers who want people back tend to cite collaboration, the training of junior staff, and buildings half empty on a lease they are still paying for. Whether those arguments are good is a separate question; the point here is structural. A survey commissioned by a recruitment firm measures what workers feel, and feelings are exactly what it is built to measure.

There is a second reason to read the numbers slowly. The 38 per cent and the 84 per cent do not share a base. The first is a share of all the workers surveyed, the second a share of the hybrid workers inside that group, and news writing compresses such distinctions because space is short. The compression is where careless reading begins: a percentage without its base is a rumour with a decimal point.

The vocabulary carries assumptions too. A "return-to-office mandate" sounds like a rule handed down, and in most of the cases described it is one. "Hybrid" sounds like a compromise, and for the workers polled it functions as the arrangement they are defending. "Wellbeing" is the softest word in the story and the one doing the most work, since it allows a dispute about money, time and autonomy to be reported as a dispute about health.

What the piece establishes, then, is narrow and useful: in the summer of 2025 a large sample of British workers said that the argument about where they work had become stressful, that the arrangement they had was good for them, and that the cost of changing it would land on their own budgets. What it does not establish is what employers should do. Those are different questions, and the article keeps them further apart than its headline does.

Here is an editor-written practice sentence, not a quotation from the original article: Were the price of the commute counted as part of the salary package, the argument over how many days a week a team should sit together would look less like a question of culture and more like a question of pay.

As you read, follow three things. First, who is speaking in each paragraph — the reporter, the poll, the statistics agency, or a named executive. Second, the base of every percentage. Third, the verbs of attribution, "said" and "according to" and "told", because a report earns its credibility from them and loses it when they blur. Then ask what the piece would need in order to become an argument rather than a report, and notice how deliberately it stays on the reporting side.

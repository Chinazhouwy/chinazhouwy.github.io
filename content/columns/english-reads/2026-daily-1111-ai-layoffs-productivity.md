# Layoffs tied to AI hurt worker productivity – and the reason may surprise managers

This page is an independently written reading guide to an article published by The Conversation on 12 August 2026. Its author is Mark Ma, Professor of Business Administration and Ben L. Fryrear Faculty Fellow at the University of Pittsburgh. The original is linked at the foot of the page. What follows traces the argument in order and explains the evidence it rests on; it is not a translation, and the wording is our own.

The article opens with a paradox rather than with a definition. Companies are putting more money into artificial intelligence than ever before, yet they are not seeing the productivity gains they expected from it. The gap between spending and result is the puzzle the piece sets out to explain.

The first move is to show that the puzzle is real and not a matter of perception. The author cites a study attributed to the Atlanta Federal Reserve in which about 90% of executives said AI had not yet raised productivity at their own companies. He also notes competing explanations for the productivity growth that has been observed since 2021 — remote work among them, and downsizing in sectors such as technology — which suggests the improvement may not be an AI effect at all.

Only then does he offer his own explanation. AI-driven layoffs, and the job insecurity that comes with them, are destroying the conditions under which AI could actually make workers more efficient. The mechanism he emphasises is sentiment: how employees feel about the technology turns out to be one of the strongest predictors of how productive a firm becomes when it uses that technology.

The practical conclusion is stated early and repeated at the end. Cutting staff in the name of AI investment is self-defeating, because it cancels out part of the gain the investment was supposed to produce. Managers and investors, the author argues, should treat that as a warning rather than as a cost of doing business.

The evidence behind the claim is a large-scale pattern study rather than an experiment. The author and his colleagues analysed millions of employee satisfaction reviews, thousands of reports of corporate financial performance, and hundreds of AI investment and layoff announcements made by US public companies over the previous five years.

The first result is a correlation. As the number of AI investment announcements rises, so does the number of announcements of job cuts attributed to AI. Two series of corporate statements move together across the sample.

The author is careful to say why he thinks the correlation is not an accident. It reflects a strategy in which reducing the workforce is treated as part of the AI programme itself, not as an unrelated economy measure that happens to coincide with it.

The logic behind that strategy is financial and short-term. Managers of listed companies are judged on whether a new investment improves profitability and the share price soon enough to matter. After a large AI investment, the pressure is to show a return quickly.

Headcount is the fastest lever available. If AI is assumed to make each employee more efficient, the same work can be done by fewer people, and cutting labour costs produces the expected saving almost immediately. Some of the companies studied went further and cut jobs before spending on AI, in order to free up the capital for it.

Markets, however, did not reward the move. When the researchers looked at share price reactions to these layoff announcements, the average return was close to zero. That is consistent with the author's earlier work, in which announcements of AI investment did not reliably lift a company's share price either.

There are exceptions. A few firms, among them the financial technology company Block, saw their shares rise on news that they would reduce staff because of AI. But across the sample, the reaction was negative or close to zero for more than half of the events.

That muted response is the clue the article follows. If the market barely reacts to a decision presented as efficiency-enhancing, the decision probably carries costs that do not appear in the announcement. The rest of the piece is an account of those costs.

The main hidden cost is fear. Employees are placed in a double bind: they are expected to adopt AI in their daily work in order to be more efficient, while the same technology is presented as a reason why their jobs may disappear.

To measure how workers respond, the researchers analysed AI-related comments in employee reviews on Glassdoor and compared their tone with the tone of reviews overall. The AI-related comments were markedly more negative.

The consequence is measured as an association, and the wording matters. The author reports a strong link between employee sentiment toward AI and firm productivity, calculated from the employers' financial information, and reads it as evidence that hostility to AI lowers productivity and offsets potential gains. Because the design is observational, the direction of cause and effect is argued rather than demonstrated.

The reviews also say what is driving the hostility. Alongside fear of losing a job to AI, employees cited a lack of proper training, few chances to upgrade their skills, weak corporate leadership on AI, and doubt about whether the tools improve productivity at all. Of these, comments about job security were the most critical by a wide margin.

The author then tests the mechanism directly. When a company announced layoffs because of AI, sentiment toward AI fell sharply. Employees appear to resist the technology partly because they have watched colleagues lose their jobs to it or expect to be next; the article supports this with a Reuters/Ipsos poll in which half of Americans said they feared AI could put someone in their household out of work.

Management sounds different. Analysing the tone of AI-related discussion in about 10,000 earnings-call transcripts, the researchers found it consistently optimistic — and found no significant relationship between that optimism and later productivity.

Hence the article's ranking of the two. What employees feel about AI matters more for extracting value from it than what executives say about it. The prescription follows: firms should make workers feel that AI is working with them, and should share the gains by investing in skills and widening opportunity instead of cutting staff. The author's image for the alternative is an “AI hunger game” that spreads fear where engagement is needed.

For a reader of English, the piece is a useful example of how a data-driven argument is assembled. It sets up a paradox, supplies a mechanism, tests the mechanism on a second body of evidence, and then states a policy conclusion. Notice, too, how carefully the causal language is graded: correlation, association, predictor, in our view. Those words mark how much the evidence can carry, and a reader who ignores them will overstate the finding.

The vocabulary is mostly the language of management and statistics: productivity, headcount, labour costs, sentiment, layoffs, correlation, observational data, return on investment. Several of them are ordinary words doing technical work — headcount means the number of employees, and sentiment means measured tone rather than a private mood.

Here is an editor-written practice sentence, not a quotation from the original article: A company that announces record spending on artificial intelligence and simultaneous redundancies is asking its remaining staff to trust the very tool it has just used against their colleagues, and that contradiction is expensive in ways the announcement does not show.

That sentence is also a fair summary of the author's position. He is not arguing that AI fails to raise productivity in principle, nor that no firm should ever reduce its workforce. He is arguing that the way the technology is introduced — and specifically whether employees experience it as a threat — determines how much of its promised value a company can actually collect.

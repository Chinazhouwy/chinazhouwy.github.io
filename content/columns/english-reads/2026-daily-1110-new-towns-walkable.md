# New towns are back – but this time they must be more walkable

This page is an independently written reading guide to an article published by The Conversation on 21 May 2026. Its authors are James White, Professor of Planning and Urban Design at the University of Glasgow, and Hannah Hickman, Associate Professor in Planning Practice at the University of the West of England. The original is linked at the foot of the page. What follows traces the argument in order and explains the ideas it depends on; it is not a translation, and the wording is our own.

The subject is a policy programme rather than a single study. The UK government has made a new generation of new towns a central part of its growth strategy, and has presented them as a way of addressing the housing shortage at the same time. The article's question is not whether the towns will be built, but whether they will be built in the form that the policy promises.

The promised form is a deliberate break with the past. The new towns built after the second world war were low-rise and depended on cars. The proposed ones are meant to be denser, more walkable, and organised around public transport and active travel. That contrast gives the article its frame: the same label, a different model of urban life.

The authors then introduce the difficulty immediately, and it is a practical one. The UK, they write, does not build dense housing particularly well, and economic, political and cultural forces are pushing new developments in the opposite direction. The rest of the piece is an account of those forces.

The government's case is stated fairly. Higher density, it argues, can support inclusive economic growth while lowering carbon emissions and reducing dependence on private cars. The new towns programme names seven proposed locations, and the article points to Leeds South Bank, Manchester Victoria North and London Thamesmead as examples of dense urban living planned where transport links and jobs already exist.

Wholly new settlements raise the question more sharply. Tempsford, in the Oxford to Cambridge growth corridor, is expected to follow a denser model than the last generation of new towns, but the policy's aim is described only as “ambitious density”. The quotation marks are the authors': the phrase is a direction rather than a specification, and the article uses it to show how much is left to be decided later.

The article then does something useful with the word density. Density, it points out, does not have to mean tall towers. Barcelona, Paris and Stockholm are built largely from six-storey apartment blocks arranged around grids of walkable streets and public squares, and they are significantly denser than comparable British cities. The image corrects an assumption that high density and high-rise are the same thing.

The implication for British housing is concrete. If the policy is serious, it requires a move away from the two-storey estates built around towns and cities in recent decades, and towards mid-rise neighbourhoods of four to eight storeys, close to mixed-use high streets that can be walked. That is a description of a built form, not a slogan, and it is where the argument becomes uncomfortable.

The first obstacle is the industry. Britain's housing system, the authors argue, is not set up to deliver these kinds of neighbourhoods or towns. Most housing is still built at low density, and the volume housebuilders who dominate the market are highly profitable; there is little appetite within those firms to adapt a business model that works for them.

The second obstacle is cost. Denser schemes can be more complex and more expensive to build. Building safety regulations, together with rising land and material costs, make high-density development less financially attractive than suburban housing. The article does not treat this as an excuse; it treats it as a reason why the promised form will not appear unless policy changes the arithmetic.

The third obstacle is preference, and here the article is careful. Research repeatedly finds that people in the UK prefer houses to flats, and proposals for denser development often meet strong local opposition. These are real constraints, but the authors do not present them as fixed. They note that such attitudes are not universal.

In much of Europe there is strong support for compact living across income groups, particularly where good local amenities and accessible green space are part of the package. Even within the UK, the three- to five-storey tenements and apartments common in Scottish cities show that higher density can be both acceptable and desirable. The British preference, in other words, may be a response to what density has usually looked like rather than a permanent fact about British people.

Recent history has made the question harder. The Grenfell Tower fire, and the revelation that thousands of people were living in buildings with the same unsafe cladding, deepened existing anxieties about higher-density urban living. The article records this without turning it into an argument against density; it treats it as part of the political weather that any new towns programme has to survive.

Language has become part of the problem. Planning ideas such as the “15-minute city” and low traffic neighbourhoods have become politically polarising, and some critics have characterised them as a government conspiracy to control how people live and move. Once a design idea acquires that kind of meaning, the technical case for it is no longer the whole argument.

The article's positive claim is a list of conditions. Dense neighbourhoods that work well, whether in Europe or in parts of the UK, depend on high-quality urban, landscape and architectural design; a mix of tenures, land uses and amenities; and good public transport. These are precisely the ingredients named in the UK's new towns policy. They are also, the authors add, the parts of placemaking that local authorities struggle to deliver and the housebuilding industry actively resists.

That sentence contains the article's real warning. The gap is not between good intentions and bad ones; it is between a policy document and the institutions that would have to carry it out. If planning deregulation leads to higher density being built hastily and without sufficient thought, the result could be crowded places without shops or services, poorly served by public transport.

The consequence would be political as well as physical. Badly built density would confirm the fears that already surround the word, and would strengthen local opposition to the next proposal. The article's closing sentence makes the test explicit: the success of the next generation of new towns will depend not just on the number of homes built, but on whether they create denser places that people genuinely want to live in.

For a reader of English, the piece is a good example of how a policy argument is assembled. Notice the pattern: a claim is stated in its strongest form, then qualified by an obstacle, then qualified again by a counter-example. Words such as but, yet, however and even so do the structural work. Remove them and the article would read as a simple attack or a simple defence; with them, it reads as an assessment.

There is also a lesson in what the article does not do. It offers almost no numbers, no cost estimates and no timetable. Its evidence is a set of examples, a policy document and a body of research on preference, and its conclusions are conditional. That is appropriate for a question whose answer depends on decisions not yet taken, but it means the reader should not mistake the article for a prediction.

Useful vocabulary here is mostly abstract: density, walkability, placemaking, tenure, deregulation, mixed use. Each names a bundle of decisions rather than a single object, and each becomes clearer when you can see which decision the sentence is actually about. The phrase “ambitious density” is a small case study in itself: an adjective doing the work that a number should do.

Here is an editor-written practice sentence, not a quotation from the original article: If a town is built at the density the policy asks for but without the shops, schools and bus routes that make density convenient, it will be judged not by the number of homes it contains but by the cars its residents still need.

That sentence is also a fair summary of the article's position. The authors are not arguing for or against new towns as such. They are arguing that density is a means whose value depends on everything built alongside it, and that the UK's record suggests the alongside is the hard part.

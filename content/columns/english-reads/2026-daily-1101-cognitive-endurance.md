# How to build kids’ ‘cognitive endurance’ in an age of distraction

This page is an independently written reading guide to an article by Heather Schofield and Supreet Kaur in Scientific American, first published online on 10 June 2026. The publisher’s full text is available at the link below.

The piece opens with a scene most readers will recognise. Halfway through a difficult exam, attention begins to slide: the words on the page blur, and the mind drifts towards what to have for dinner. The authors’ point is that this is not a failure of character. It is a window into how minds behave under sustained demand.

Both writers are behavioural scientists who study how economic circumstances shape thinking, and the article is built around a term they want readers to take seriously: cognitive endurance. The phrase is deliberately physical. Just as a runner slows over a long race, almost everyone performs worse on a task the longer they keep at it.

That claim rests on a study of more than 1,600 children, and the pattern held almost everywhere the researchers looked: scores fell as time on task grew. What varied was the steepness of the fall.

The variation is where the argument turns social. Children in poor countries declined about three times as fast as children in wealthy ones, and students from disadvantaged backgrounds slipped more quickly than better-off peers. The authors then describe what those school days actually contained. In wealthier schools, children were more often left to work through problems alone, to read silently, or to concentrate on a single task; in poorer ones, much of the day went on listening to lectures, memorising by rote and copying from the board.

That contrast suggested a hypothesis: the school day itself, and the amount of sustained effort it demands, may be training the capacity in question — or failing to train it.

To test the idea, the researchers ran an experiment with 1,636 elementary school students in India. Study-hall periods were assigned to one of three conditions. The control group carried on as usual, copying a few maths problems from the board and then filling the rest of the time as they wished, which required very little continuous effort.

The two treatment groups spent twenty minutes of the same period on unbroken cognitive work. One solved maths problems on tablets, in an application that adapted to each child’s level but deliberately offered no game-like rewards. The other played demanding games — mazes and tangrams — with no academic content at all, also calibrated to keep them challenged.

Both groups improved, and by almost the same amount. On tests of listening comprehension, reasoning and maths, performance declined about 22 per cent more slowly than in the control group. Whether the practice was mathematical or not made little difference, which supports the authors’ reading: the act of concentrating mattered more than the subject being concentrated on.

The gains appeared on other measures as well. Treated students did better on standard tests of sustained attention, such as spotting a target symbol in a grid; their teachers reported less fidgeting and better follow-through on multi-step instructions; and their grades in Hindi, English and maths were roughly 0.09 standard deviations higher.

To give that figure a scale, the authors compare it with a well-known intervention: placing a pupil in a class with seven fewer students per teacher. The cognitive-training effect was between half and three quarters as large — for twenty to fifty minutes a week over six months.

The article then leaves the classroom. In related work, data-entry workers made more mistakes as their shifts wore on, and the decline was steeper for those with less education. Voting shows something similar: when a proposition appears further down a long ballot in California, voters are more likely to leave the default option in place, and the effect is strongest in lower-income neighbourhoods.

The implication is uncomfortable. If the ability to keep thinking hard is partly built through practice, and if access to that practice is unevenly distributed, then a gap that opens at school may widen for decades. Endurance becomes one more channel through which early disadvantage compounds.

The authors are careful about what they have not settled. They do not know which form of training works best, and they note that the experiment measured short-run gains in one setting. Their practical suggestion is broad rather than prescriptive: puzzles, a musical instrument, certain video games — anything that demands sustained, deliberate and self-directed effort.

What stays with the reader is the framing. Attention is treated here less as a fixed gift than as stamina, something that can be spent, trained and exhausted; the closing lines insist that the capacity is not fixed.

Here is an editor-written practice sentence, not a quotation from the original article: Because the study rewarded nothing but persistence, its results suggest that what children practise may matter less than the habit of staying with a task until it is finished.

As you read, keep the study and the argument apart. The experiment was randomised and the outcome measured, so the causal claim about practice is stronger than the correlational claims about schools and countries; notice the hedged verbs — “may”, “seems”, “suggest” — and the comparison with class size, which is offered as a yardstick rather than as proof that the two are equivalent.

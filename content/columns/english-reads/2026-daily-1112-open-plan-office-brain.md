# Why your brain has to work harder in an open-plan office than private offices: study

This page is an independently written reading guide to an article published by The Conversation on 15 February 2026. Its author is Libby (Elizabeth) Sander, MBA Director and Associate Professor of Organisational Behaviour at Bond Business School, Bond University. The original is linked at the foot of the page. What follows traces the argument in order and explains the evidence it rests on; it is not a translation, and the wording is our own.

The starting point is a change in office space rather than in office habits. Since the pandemic, many organisations have decided that they need less floor space and fewer desks, because staff now split their week between home and the workplace. The result is a familiar pattern: on the days when people are required to come in, the same floor is noticeably fuller and noisier than it used to be.

The article notices an imbalance in that debate. A great deal of attention has gone to how often employees should be in the office, and much less to what they return to when they arrive. The open-plan layout, which was already common before 2020, has survived the shift to hybrid work largely unexamined. The question the author asks is therefore narrow and practical: does the physical arrangement of a workplace change the mental effort that ordinary office tasks require?

To answer it, she turns to a recently published study in the journal Buildings, carried out by researchers at a Spanish university. The sample was small: 26 people, ranging from their mid-twenties to their mid-sixties. Each participant wore a wireless electroencephalogram headset, a device that records electrical activity across the scalp and can therefore indicate how hard the brain is working.

The tasks were designed to resemble a normal working day rather than a laboratory puzzle. Participants monitored notifications, read and answered emails, and memorised lists of words that they were later asked to recall. These are the kinds of activities that fill an office morning, and they place different demands on attention, memory and the ability to switch between them.

Each person completed the same tasks in two settings. The first was an open-plan workspace with colleagues nearby. The second was a small enclosed work pod, with clear glazed panels on one side. Because every participant experienced both conditions, the comparison controls for differences between individuals: the study is asking what changes when the surroundings change, not whether quiet people perform better than sociable ones.

The researchers concentrated on the frontal regions of the brain, which support planning, attention and the filtering of distractions, and they compared several frequency bands. The article borrows a short explainer from the neuroscientist Susan Hillier to describe them. Gamma activity is associated with focused concentration; beta with more active, externally directed attention and with higher anxiety; alpha with relaxed, passive attention; theta with deep relaxation and inward focus; delta with deep sleep. The study itself treats theta as a marker that tracks both working memory and mental fatigue, which is why its rise later in the piece matters.

Those labels are broad associations rather than direct readings of effort, and the article uses them carefully. The important point is not that one band is good and another bad, but that the pattern across bands differs between the two rooms. A brain that is working harder to hold the same task should show a different signature from one that is settling into it.

Inside the pod, the signature moved in the direction of ease. Beta activity, tied to active mental processing, fell significantly over the course of the experiment. Alpha activity, tied to passive attention, fell as well, and overall activity in the frontal regions declined. The interpretation offered is that participants needed progressively less effort to sustain the same work as the session went on.

In the open-plan condition the pattern reversed. Gamma activity, associated with complex mental processing, climbed steadily. Theta activity increased. Two broader measures also rose significantly: arousal, meaning how alert and activated the brain is, and engagement, meaning how much mental effort is being applied. On the article's reading, participants in the open office had to work harder simply to maintain their performance.

The explanation is not that open-plan offices make people lazy or that noise is unpleasant in some vague way. It is that ignoring a distraction is itself a task. Background conversation, a colleague walking past, a phone ringing two desks away: each has to be noticed, classified as irrelevant and set aside, and that filtering draws on the same limited resources that the main task needs. The pod removed most of that background noise and visual movement, so the brain could spend less on filtering and more on the work.

The study also found much wider variation in the open-plan setting. Some participants showed a dramatic increase in frontal activity; others changed only modestly. This is a useful detail, because it suggests that the cost of an open office is not distributed evenly. People differ in how much they are distracted by nearby activity, and a design that suits one person may be expensive for another.

The author is candid about the limits of the evidence. Twenty-six participants is a small sample, and a single experiment cannot settle a question about years of working life. Rather than presenting the result as decisive, she places it within a body of earlier research that points in the same direction, and she treats the convergence as the stronger argument.

Her own earlier work is part of that body. In a 2021 study, she and her colleagues tested 43 participants under controlled conditions, combining heart rate, skin conductivity and facial emotion recognition. They reported that negative mood in open-plan offices rose by 25 per cent and physiological stress by 34 per cent. The measures are different from brain waves, but the conclusion is similar: the environment registers in the body, not only in opinion surveys.

Other studies fill in the picture. One found that background conversations and noisy conditions degrade performance on cognitive tasks and increase distraction. A much larger analysis, published in 2013, looked at more than 42,000 office workers in the United States, Finland, Canada and Australia and found that those in open-plan offices were less satisfied with their work environment than those in private offices. The main reasons given were uncontrollable noise and a lack of privacy.

The article's most effective analogy is with furniture. Poorly designed chairs are now widely recognised as a cause of physical strain, and few employers would defend them as a cost-saving measure. Workspace design, the author suggests, deserves the same scrutiny, because it can produce cognitive strain that is less visible but just as real. The comparison quietly changes the status of the issue: it moves office layout out of the category of taste and into the category of occupational health.

That matters because uninterrupted concentration is not a peripheral activity in modern work. It is the basic requirement of knowledge work: reading, writing, analysing, deciding. The article's complaint is that this requirement is systematically undervalued when workplaces are designed, even though the same organisations often say that focused work is what they most need from their employees.

The prescription follows from the diagnosis. If the problem is that one room is being asked to support incompatible tasks, the answer is to create zones where the environment matches the task. Acoustic treatment, sound-masking technology and thoughtfully placed partitions can reduce both auditory and visual interruption. None of these is exotic; they are standard elements of office design that are often omitted when the priority is density and cost per desk.

The article offers one concrete example. LinkedIn redesigned its flagship San Francisco office for hybrid work and halved the number of workstations in its open-plan areas. In their place it experimented with 75 types of work setting, including areas reserved for quiet focus. The point is not that every organisation should copy a technology company, but that a large employer with an interest in density still found reasons to provide a range of environments.

The financial argument is acknowledged rather than avoided. Adding acoustic features, partitions and additional zones can cost more up front than laying out a single open floor. The author's response is that the comparison is incomplete, because the savings are visible in the budget while the costs are spread across productivity, health and staff retention. Research on poor office design, she notes, has documented a significant hidden toll in all three.

The conclusion is a claim about status rather than about furniture. Giving employees some choice over how much noise and interruption they are exposed to is not a luxury or a perk. If organisations want more done with less strain, better design at work should be treated as a necessity. The sentence is short, and it deliberately reframes the debate: the question is not whether quiet space is nice to have, but whether the absence of it is a predictable cost.

For a reader of English, the article is a good example of how a small study can be used responsibly. Notice the order of the moves: the finding is stated, its sample size is conceded, earlier evidence is added, and only then is a recommendation made. The word "but" does a lot of work in the middle of the piece, usually introducing a limit that the author accepts before continuing.

It is also worth noticing what the study measures. The dependent variable is neural effort, not output: the researchers can say that the brain worked harder, not that the work was worse. The article mostly respects that distinction, which is why it speaks of strain and of the cost of filtering rather than claiming that open offices make people less productive. A careless summary would blur the two, and the difference is exactly what a careful reader should keep.

The vocabulary is largely the vocabulary of workplaces and of neuroscience, and the two sets interlock. Open-plan, pod, zoning, acoustic treatment, sound masking and partition describe the physical setting; arousal, engagement, gamma, beta and theta describe the body's response to it. Learning the pairs is more useful than learning either list alone, because the article's argument is precisely that the first set produces the second.

Here is an editor-written practice sentence, not a quotation from the original article: A room that saves money on partitions may spend it later on the effort its occupants need to ignore one another.

That sentence compresses the article's economics, and it also shows how a conditional structure can carry a judgement. The main clause makes a claim about spending; the relative clause explains where the cost comes from; the modal "may" keeps the claim proportionate. The article's own conclusion is stronger than a hope and weaker than a law, and this is roughly the tone it maintains throughout.

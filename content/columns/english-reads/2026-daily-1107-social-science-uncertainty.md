# The Guardian view on social science research: embracing uncertainty

This page is an independently written reading guide to an editorial published by The Guardian on 15 April 2026. The paper labels the piece “Editorial” and names no individual author, so the byline belongs to the newspaper's editorial board rather than to a single journalist. The original article is linked at the foot of the page. What follows is a guide to the argument and to the way it handles numbers, written for readers who want to take the English slowly; it is not a translation of the piece.

The editorial opens with a figure that sounds like an alarm. A new set of studies, it says, suggests that as many as half of all results published in reputable social science journals cannot be replicated by independent analysis. Two words in that sentence are doing most of the work. “As many as” marks an upper bound rather than a typical case, and “replicated” has a technical meaning that the piece goes on to define. A reader who skips both will carry away a stronger claim than the evidence supports.

The problem, the editorial is careful to say, is neither new nor confined to one discipline. It has been running for years across many research fields, most visibly in the social sciences and psychology, with concerns also raised in parts of biomedical research. The framing matters: this is presented as a standing feature of how research is done and checked, not as a scandal that arrived with a particular paper or a particular laboratory.

The evidence behind the opening number comes from a seven-year project with an unwieldy name — Systematizing Confidence in Open Research and Evidence, shortened to Score. It has published three studies covering 3,900 social science papers. Its findings include a pattern worth remembering: newer papers, and papers in journals that require extensive sharing of the underlying data, were more likely to be reproduced. That is a claim about conditions rather than about virtue — transparency and recency travel together with reproducibility.

Medicine gets a paragraph of its own, and it complicates the picture in a useful way. Clinical research has its own constraints: patient caseloads differ, samples are limited, and a trial in one hospital system is not the same object as a trial in another. In practice, the editorial suggests, medical research can resemble the social sciences more closely than it resembles laboratory physics. The practical conclusion is modest and sensible: policymakers should be cautious about claims that lack a wide and robust base of evidence.

Then comes the distinction on which the whole argument rests. Reproducibility asks whether results can be recreated from the same data and the same methods. Replication asks whether a finding holds when new data are gathered in different contexts. The two failures mean different things. A reanalysis that cannot recover a published number points to an error or an undisclosed choice; a replication that fails may be describing a world that has changed, or a boundary the first study never tested.

Science, the editorial notes, rarely produces exactly identical outcomes, and working out why is part of how knowledge accumulates. The trouble is political. Uncertainty of this ordinary kind is easily converted into denial, and normal scientific caution gets recast as evidence of failure. The editorial points to a White House executive order from May 2025 that emphasised a “reproducibility crisis” in science, and reads it as a call for doubt and inaction rather than for better research.

If verification matters, why is there so little of it? The answer is structural and unglamorous. Large-scale verification projects such as Score are few and far between, because most academic researchers would rather spend their time on work that is more likely to advance their careers. Checking someone else's result is expensive, slow and hard to publish; the incentive runs the other way.

The numbers sharpen the point. Score reanalysed existing data and, in separate work, replicated studies from scratch across more than 100 papers. Around 49 per cent still failed to replicate the original result — a figure close enough to half to keep the opening claim honest, and hedged by the same “around”. The asymmetry is the lesson: reanalysing data is relatively straightforward, while carrying out an identical experiment is not, especially where outcomes depend on complex human systems. The editorial adds a caution about artificial intelligence: it may help decide what to test, but it cannot reduce the cost or the time of duplicating a piece of research.

Having built the case, the editorial refuses the easy ending. Not every failed replication signals a crisis. Some findings do not matter much; replication studies can themselves be flawed. Results that do not replicate consistently should be weighed against a wider evidence base when policy is being guided, rather than treated as a verdict on a whole field.

The sentence at the centre of the piece is a definitional one: treating non-replication as disqualification confuses uncertainty with ignorance. The two are not the same, and the confusion has consequences. If every uncertain finding is discarded, the decision-maker is left not with better evidence but with no evidence at all, and paralysis arrives dressed as rigour.

The remedy the editorial proposes is procedural rather than heroic. Greater transparency makes outright fraud more difficult and allows errors to be identified, and some major funders already require it — the UK Economic and Social Research Council is named. The editorial's view is that the practice should be universal, which is a modest demand with an immodest price, since it asks institutions to publish the material that makes them checkable.

There is also a camp that is unbothered, arguing that research ultimately autocorrects. The editorial grants the point and then qualifies it. The long-term solution — shifting incentives so that existing results get tested — would indeed increase confidence, but it requires restructuring research culture and funding, and for now it remains largely notional. The studies are presented as both a warning and an argument for that change.

For a reader working on English, the piece is a short lesson in quantification. Watch “as many as”, “around”, “more likely” and “largely”: each one adjusts the strength of the claim it sits in, and each one is the part of a sentence a hurried reader drops. Watch, too, how the editorial uses the same root in three forms — reproduce, replicate, reanalyse — with three different meanings, which is exactly the sort of vocabulary cluster worth owning.

Notice as well how much of the argument is carried by nominalisation. “Verification”, “transparency”, “replication” and “disqualification” are actions turned into nouns, which lets the writer stack them into subjects and keep the sentences compact. English opinion writing does this constantly; unpacking a noun back into a verb — to verify, to disclose, to disqualify — is often the fastest way to see what a dense sentence is actually claiming.

Here is an editor-written practice sentence, not a quotation from the original article: Had the funders required data sharing a decade earlier, the replication studies published this month would have had more to work with, and the researchers who spent years checking old results might have been paid for that work instead of fitting it around it.

It is worth being explicit about what the editorial does not do. It names no individual paper, offers no estimate of how much irreproducible research costs, and does not claim that fraud is widespread; its target is the machinery of checking and the incentives around it. Nor does it deny that some published findings are simply wrong. The argument is narrower and more useful: a field that measures its own uncertainty is in better health than one that hides it, and a reader who keeps the qualifiers can tell the difference between a crisis and a working science.

The editorial ends by calling social science a powerful tool for understanding the world, and by locating trust in acknowledgement rather than repudiation. That is a short sentence with a long reach. The same habit serves anyone reading a study, a news report about a study, or a politician quoting one: find the claim, find its scope, and ask what would have to be true for it to fail.

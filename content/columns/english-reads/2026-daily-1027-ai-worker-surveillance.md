# Forget the AI job apocalypse. AI’s real threat is worker control and surveillance

This page is an independently written reading guide to a Guardian analysis by Nazrul Islam, first published online on 11 May 2026. Follow the original-article link below for the publisher’s full text.

The article argues that the usual debate about AI and work is framed in the wrong terms. Warnings about mass unemployment and promises of a productivity boom both direct attention away from changes that are already visible in workplaces in Britain, Kenya and the United States.

For employees in well-paid, high-autonomy roles such as analysts, consultants, lawyers, academics and managers, the technology can work as a helper. When it is introduced to support rather than replace them, it can take over routine tasks, leave more room for judgement and free time for creative work.

The picture is different for many lower-paid workers, the author writes. There, AI often arrives as scheduling software, route optimisation, monitoring tools and automated performance dashboards — systems that allocate shifts, set expected task times and grade output. The article describes this as a divide between people who use AI and people who are managed by it, and notes that about a third of UK employers already use monitoring technology often called bossware.

The analysis warns that these methods are moving up the labour market. It cites Amazon software engineers who report being monitored and pressed to use AI to raise productivity, and Meta’s reported plans to record employees’ keystrokes, mouse movements and clicks as training data. Workers who currently benefit from AI may therefore face the same oversight later.

Drawing on the author’s decade of research on workers and AI, which the article says was cited in a 2024 White House economic report, the piece locates the main problem less in immediate job losses than in a widening gap in skills, autonomy and wellbeing. Because work provides dignity, trust and control as well as income, constant measurement by systems that workers cannot see or challenge becomes a source of stress; the effect is felt most in warehousing, retail, hospitality, logistics, customer service and gig work.

The closing section turns to policy. Britain is expanding AI training programmes, but the article cites a survey of business leaders suggesting that few organisations commit serious budgets to developing employees’ skills or put strong governance in place. It calls for training in judgement, communication and critical thinking, for performance systems that are transparent and open to challenge, and for workers to have a voice in how AI is introduced — a process the article says research links to better job quality and more effective adoption.

Here is an editor-written practice sentence, not a quotation from the original article: Whether AI feels like a copilot or a supervisor depends less on the technology itself than on who gets to set the terms of its use.

As you read, notice how the article separates two claims that are often merged: that AI may change how many jobs exist, and that it is already changing how work is directed and judged. Track the contrast between using a tool and being assessed by one, and pay attention to the evidence the author offers for each stage of the argument.

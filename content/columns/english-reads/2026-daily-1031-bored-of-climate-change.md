# Bored of Climate Change

This page is an independently written reading guide to a post by James Danckert and John Eastwood on the Psychology Today blog The Engaged Mind, first published online on 18 July 2024. Follow the original-article link below for the publisher’s full text.

The post begins with a book. Rachel Carson published *Silent Spring* in 1962, and it is usually treated as the moment when the harm done by pesticides, and by extension by human activity in general, entered ordinary conversation. The authors are careful to add that scientists had been worrying about human effects on the climate since at least the early nineteenth century; what changed in the 1960s was not the evidence but its audience.

From there the piece sets out its puzzle. Readers today are not short of information: there are regular reports from the international panel on climate change, and wildfires, floods and heat waves lead the news. Yet the response still looks slow, and the authors ask why the willingness to act is so much weaker than the flow of warnings would lead one to expect.

Part of the answer, they concede, is organised self-interest. Some industries have worked to shift responsibility onto individuals while lobbying governments to slow regulation, and people who hold more conservative political views tend to describe the problem with less urgency. But the post is after something more basic: the emotions that decide whether a person acts at all.

Emotions, in this account, do not point in a single direction. Anxiety about the future can push someone to do something in order to relieve the stress of thinking about it, or it can push them to withdraw. Helplessness is equally double-edged: if the private verdict is "what is the point?", inaction follows, but a person who concludes that the problem needs collective effort may be moved to join a larger campaign rather than give up.

The evidence comes from a survey paper by Nathanial Geiger and colleagues. Participants were asked how strongly they felt anxiety, helplessness, hope and boredom, and then how likely they were to take part in what the researchers call public sphere actions — encouraging other people to act, passing on what they know, giving money. These are the actions that spread a response beyond the household.

Boredom is the emotion the authors spend most time on, and they offer several reasons why it should matter. One is anticipation: if a task looks dull before it starts, it becomes harder to picture yourself doing it, and harder still to commit. Another is scale. When the only steps a person can imagine taking are small ones, such as sorting plastics, those steps can look both pointless and tedious at the same time.

A third reason is the way bad news is delivered. The authors compare climate coverage to the shock campaigns once used against smoking and, in Australia in the 1990s, against drink-driving, where blunt images and slogans confronted people with the consequences of their behaviour. The comparison is not flattering: audiences get used to shock, and what once stopped people in their tracks can end up merely familiar, or boring.

They cite a study of cigarette warnings over nine years in Canada and the United States. The warnings lost effectiveness in both countries, but the decline was steepest in Canada, where packets carried pictures rather than words alone. The more shocking the warning, the shorter its useful life appeared to be.

Boredom also carries information about control. The authors describe it as a signal that a person needs agency, a sense of deciding for themselves what happens next. Climate advice presses directly on that nerve, because it tells people what to do and adds that their past choices helped create the problem. Meanwhile people who are strongly boredom-prone already report a weaker sense of agency, and the same trait is associated with lower participation in politics and voting, which are public sphere actions of exactly this kind.

Distance does the rest. In Canada, where the authors work, the effects of a warming climate can still feel abstract: less snow in winter, fewer days when outdoor rinks stay frozen. Those changes are real but remote from daily life, and where personal connection is thin, the sense of meaning thins with it, leaving more room for boredom.

The results of the survey were narrower than the range of explanations. Anxiety and helplessness turned out not to be major predictors of intent to act. Boredom was. People who felt more bored at the prospect of acting were less likely to say they intended to. Boredom and helplessness also did not reinforce each other, which counts against the simplest story, in which boredom works only by producing despair.

Hope was the strongest positive predictor of all. That is the constructive half of the argument: a person acts when a route to the goal looks both worthwhile and reachable, so the practical task is less about frightening people than about making the available steps visible and credible. The authors are candid that the survey measured stated intentions rather than behaviour, and that how to manufacture hope at scale remains an open question.

Here is an editor-written practice sentence, not a quotation from the original article: People who assume in advance that an effort will be dull find it harder to imagine themselves making it, and that failure of imagination is easily mistaken for evidence that the effort would change nothing.

As you read, watch how the post separates three things: what the survey measured, what the authors think it explains, and what they admit they cannot yet show. Notice how much of the argument rests on hedged wording — "may", "might", "appears" — and how a comparison with cigarette advertising is used as an analogy rather than as proof.

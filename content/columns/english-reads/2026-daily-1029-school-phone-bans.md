# ‘Close to zero impact’: US study casts doubt on effect of phone ban in schools

This page is an independently written reading guide to a news report by Richard Adams in The Guardian, first published online on 5 May 2026. Follow the original-article link below for the publisher’s full text.

England is moving towards a legal requirement that state schools keep phones away from students during the school day, and the case for that step has rested largely on a simple hope: take the devices out of students’ hands and learning will improve. A large American study reported in May put that hope to the test, and its answer is uncomfortable for everyone in the argument.

The study followed nearly 1,800 secondary schools in the United States that had adopted a strict version of the policy: students’ phones were sealed in magnetic locked pouches for the school day. The researchers compared these schools with similar ones that had no such rule, looking at standardised test scores and at non-academic measures such as attendance, attention in class and students’ own reports of online bullying. The findings were issued as a working paper by the National Bureau of Economic Research.

On the central question the results were flat. Average effects on test scores were close to zero, and the estimates were precise enough that the null result is hard to dismiss as statistical noise. Attendance, classroom attention and perceived online bullying showed effects that were similarly small. The averages did hide some variation — older students gained modestly in maths while younger pupils did slightly worse — but neither shift was large.

The first year of a ban was harder than the years that followed. Suspensions rose and students’ sense of wellbeing dipped, apparently as schools worked out how to enforce the new rule. Those effects then faded, and wellbeing later rose above its level before the ban.

What the policy did change was behaviour. Using location data from the phones themselves, the researchers found a substantial fall in phone activity by the third year of a ban. Students really were using their phones far less; the improvement simply did not appear in the outcomes the study was able to measure.

Thomas Dee of Stanford University, one of the authors, argued that a mixed picture is not a reason to abandon the policy. He told NPR that reading the results as a case for walking away would be a serious mistake, pointing to the falling phone use and to signs that schools with longer experience of bans were beginning to record more positive results.

In England the report has landed in the middle of a live argument. The children’s commissioner has found that more than nine in ten schools already restrict phones in some form, and the new statutory rules are expected to be stricter, requiring phones to be handed in or locked away. The Girls’ Day School Trust, which runs 25 schools, will bar phones for pupils up to year 11 from September, citing evidence about harm to girls in particular. Separately, the exam regulator has warned that more than 500 candidates were disqualified last year for taking phones or smart watches into exam halls.

Read the piece with two distinctions in mind. The first is between no measurable average effect and no effect at all: a policy can change daily behaviour, as the location data show, without moving the particular numbers a study is able to collect. The second is between short-run disruption and long-run results, since the first year of any ban looks worse than the years that follow it.

Here is an editor-written practice sentence, not a quotation from the original article: Even where a policy changes behaviour in the way its supporters hoped, the outcomes that matter most may take years to show up in the data — and they may never show up in the measures a study happens to choose.

As you read, notice how the report keeps three things separate: what the study measured, what the authors concluded, and what politicians and schools decided to do. Pay attention to the careful verbs — “casts doubt on”, “close to zero”, “modest” — which do far more work here than any single figure, and watch how the closing paragraphs move from evidence to policy without claiming that the evidence settles the argument.

# How mental exhaustion changes the choices you make

This page is an independently written reading guide to a first-person Scientific American article by researchers at Johns Hopkins University and the Kennedy Krieger Institute, first published online on 28 July 2026. Follow the original-article link below for the publisher’s full text.

The article opens with a scene many parents will recognise. After a whole day of making breakfast, settling arguments, answering questions and keeping children occupied, a parent finally sits down to decide what to cook — and even that ordinary decision, with its questions about ingredients, cooking time and whether the food will be eaten at all, feels like too much.

The author gives this experience a name: cognitive fatigue. It is not the same as physical tiredness, and it turns up well beyond family life. An office worker who has spent the day on repetitive computer tasks may feel unable to go out with friends, and a nurse finishing a long shift may cancel an extra visit to a patient even though the visit had been planned.

To study what happens in the brain at such moments, the team scanned 28 volunteers with functional magnetic resonance imaging. Participants first worked through demanding memory exercises, then chose between two ways of earning money: an easy test that paid one dollar for a correct answer, or a harder test that could pay up to eight dollars. The cash amounts were there to give a precise price to people’s willingness to work harder.

The result was consistent. As participants grew more fatigued, they became much less likely to choose the demanding test, even though it offered a larger reward. Effort, in other words, was quietly becoming more expensive in their minds.

The scans pointed to a specific circuit. During the exhausting exercises, the dorsolateral prefrontal cortex, an area at the front of the brain, sent increasingly strong signals to the right anterior insula, a region associated with monitoring the body’s internal state, including fatigue. The stronger the traffic between the two areas, the more likely a person was to settle for the easier option. Because the pattern could be measured, the researchers were able to use it to estimate how each volunteer traded mental effort against reward.

The findings matter beyond the laboratory. The article notes that severe mental exhaustion is a prominent feature of depression, long COVID and multiple sclerosis, and suggests that identifying these circuits could eventually help in designing treatments. The author is careful to say that much about severe fatigue remains unknown.

The closing part of the article offers an evolutionary reading. Fatigue signals probably developed to stop us from overdrawing on our mental and physical reserves; when those signals are amplified, extra effort simply feels costlier. Yet the same signals can also block activities that would restore us, such as a walk outside after work, and they can stand in the way of worthwhile tasks like preparing a healthy meal. Research cited in the article suggests that understanding why an activity matters, or finding it genuinely interesting, can help people push past fatigue — in other words, reframing the task can change what we are willing to do.

Here is an editor-written practice sentence, not a quotation from the original article: The more depleted the brain feels, the steeper the price it puts on effort, so a reward that once looked attractive can suddenly seem not worth the trouble.

As you read, watch how the article moves from everyday examples to a laboratory design and then back to ordinary life. Notice that the study does not claim people become unable to work when tired, only that their internal accounting of cost and reward shifts, and pay attention to the careful wording the author uses when discussing clinical conditions.
